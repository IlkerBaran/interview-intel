# 0014. Classifier Retrain: Label Redesign, Evidence-Based Urgency, Derived Abstention

## Status

Accepted

## Context

The three classifiers were trained on a synthetic corpus and evaluated on a held-out slice of it. They scored 1.000 on that slice and roughly 0.35 on real recruiting email. The gap was large and came from the data rather than the model.

The corpus was 866 rows generated from 72 templates — about twelve fills each — with a median length of 34 words and no subject lines. Real pasted email runs past 80 words with a subject, a signature and a logistics paragraph. Measured against the training vocabulary, the median real email was 30% out-of-vocabulary where a training row was 5.3%: a third of every real email was invisible to the model. `python`, `sql`, `internship`, `system design`, `take-home`, `hiring manager` and `new grad` appeared zero times in 866 rows; `backend` appeared in 7 and `frontend` in 1.

Three further faults compounded it. The `job_field` class `general` was generated by blanking the role and company placeholders, so it occupied the template skeleton rather than any semantic region and absorbed anything unfamiliar — 0.37 precision against 0.78 recall. There was no category for an application confirmation, so those emails landed on `rejection` at 0.50 confidence; after the stage gating in `GUIDANCE_POLICY` that meant a confirmation produced no guidance, no questions and no reply. And `urgency` was drawn from `random.choices(urgency_weights[category])`, assigned by category and not recoverable from the email text: a majority-per-category baseline scored 0.781 against a trained 0.758, so the head had not learned meaningful urgency signal and was fitting candidate names.

ADR-0013's evaluation work (grouped splitting, held-out selection) had already made the evaluation honest. It did not improve the underlying data.

## Decision

Regenerate the corpus with real recruiting vocabulary, replace both label sets, derive urgency from stated evidence, and derive the abstain threshold per model.

### The corpus stays synthetic, and that is a known limitation

There is no real corpus. The evaluation set is four hand-labelled emails (`ml/data/real_emails.csv`), which is not enough to conclude anything from. Improving the synthetic data was what was achievable; it cannot be validated against the distribution it will actually meet. Every number below that comes from synthetic data describes a task this project constructed and then solved; none predicts real-world accuracy.

The corpus is now 1,848 rows from 154 templates (22 per category), median 84 words, 79% carrying a subject line. Median real-email OOV fell from 30.0% to 7.4% — per row 12.7%, 15.8%, 2.2%, 2.3%. The seven previously-absent terms now appear in 34, 47, 37, 41, 121, 199 and 49 rows respectively; `backend` in 33 and `frontend` in 82.

### `general` is removed rather than repaired

Because it was built by blanking placeholders, `general` absorbed anything out-of-vocabulary, which is precisely the failure mode being fixed. Repairing it would have meant generating genuinely field-neutral prose rather than field-stripped prose — a real class competing for probability mass with the eleven that matter. Below-threshold is a decision about confidence, not a category, so it belongs outside the label set.

The eleven fields are all technical. Below the threshold `ml_service` now writes `"unclassified"` (`JOB_FIELD_UNCLASSIFIED`), a value the model can never emit, which stays distinct from `NULL` — `NULL` means ML did not run at all. The Field badge therefore has three unambiguous states where it previously had two overlapping ones, since "General" on screen could mean either "predicted general" or "was unsure".

### Fields are tech-only, with two deliberate collapses

Finance, marketing and operations are gone; the audience is engineers and CS students, and fewer classes means more rows each. Two of the eleven are deliberate merges of boundaries that are genuinely blurry in real mail.

`software_engineering` absorbs backend work and unqualified titles. An email naming "Software Engineer Intern" with no framework mentioned is `software_engineering` **by definition** — that is the correct answer, not a choice between it and the specialisms.

`data_related` absorbs analyst, scientist and data engineer. Real postings use these interchangeably and the interview loops overlap heavily.

`fullstack` is the class most at risk of becoming an undecidable superset of `software_engineering` plus `frontend`, so the generator enforces a rule: **never emit a `fullstack` row that is merely backend tokens plus frontend tokens with no explicit marker.** Every `fullstack` row carries a role title containing "full stack"/"fullstack", a named fullstack stack (MERN, Next.js with Prisma), or a both-sides phrase. Without that the boundary would be undecidable for a human reader too.

### One template serves all eleven fields

A template is written once and filled for every field; the field enters *only* through the vocabulary substituted into `{role}`, `{tech}`, `{topic}` and `{format}`. This forces the field head to learn terminology rather than phrasing, and it is why grouping the train/test split by `template_id` (ADR-0013) leaks no field information.

The same reasoning governs the shared pools. Names, companies, seniority and process vocabulary are sampled independently of every label, because the previous model's top features for `general` included `conversation yamamoto` and `link sato` — randomly assigned candidate names that correlated by chance. Company names were a second instance with a real cause: per-field company lists made `hugging face` predict data science. Companies now use one shared pool, and the names were rewritten to remove technology words after `Kestrel Data` and `Quarry Software` were found scattering field tokens across unrelated rows.

Shared lines must be genuinely label-neutral, so this was verified explicitly. A first draft of the shared courtesy pool included "I'll keep you updated at each stage" — forward-looking process language that contradicts a rejection outright. It affected category performance: rejection recall fell to 0.47 with 21 rejections read as `application_received`. Removing those lines and making two vague rejection templates explicit moved rejection F1 from 0.643 to 0.733.

### `application_received` becomes a first-class category

The seventh category needed new templates rather than a relabel, because no existing template distinguished "we have your application" from "checking in on your application".

It is wired through `MessageCategory`, `GUIDANCE_POLICY` (`prep=True, questions=False, reply=False` — someone who has just applied still wants to know what to do while waiting, but there is no interviewer to ask questions of and an automated acknowledgement has nothing to reply to), `SIMILAR_CATEGORY_GROUPS`, the badge macro and the filter dropdown.

`TASK_RULES` and `STATUS_MAP` are deliberately not extended. An acknowledgement warrants no task, and a `STATUS_MAP` entry would be a no-op as `_auto_update_status` stands — `ACTIVE_APPLICATION_STATUSES` excludes `SAVED` and the forward-only rank check blocks `APPLIED → APPLIED` — so it would appear to do something without changing anything.

### Urgency is derived from a stated deadline

Dropping the head was the alternative. It was kept because urgency is useful and is genuinely present in the text; what was missing was a connection between the label and that text.

The label is now defined so a human reader can decide it from the email alone. If a reader cannot recover it, neither can a model:

1. Does the email ask the reader to do anything? No → **low**.
2. Is there a deadline inside about a week, or an explicit time-critical phrase? Yes → **high**. No → **medium**.

Low also covers optional asks ("reply only if you're interested"): no deadline, nothing time-bound. Every row carries one marker sentence drawn from a category-agnostic pool matching its drawn label, and the label is drawn *before* the email is built so the label and its evidence are the same fact rather than two independent draws. A row without a marker would be an unlabelled example.

Per-category weights were flattened so every category spans at least two levels with real mass. The zeros are semantic rather than cosmetic: an invitation, a scheduling email and an offer always ask the reader to do something, so `low` is unreachable for them; a rejection and an automated acknowledgement never impose a deadline, so `high` is unreachable for those. Category still narrows the answer to two of three — that is real and unavoidable — but no longer determines it. The majority-per-category baseline fell from 0.781 to 0.573.

### The urgency rule becomes a floor, not an override

`HIGH_URGENCY_CATEGORIES` forced `urgency = "high"` for every interview invitation and offer regardless of the text. That made the badge mean "interview" rather than "act soon", and after this change it would have discarded the model's answer on exactly the two categories where urgency now varies most.

`URGENCY_FLOOR_CATEGORIES` lifts only when the model says `low`, and only to `medium`. A model reading of medium or high stands untouched. This keeps the safety goal — not missing a time-sensitive interview email — without flattening the distinction.

`urgency_source` follows: `"rule_based"` is retired for urgency, because under a floor the model is always consulted and naming a rule when the floor did not fire would record an inaccurate source in the `AgentRun` audit log. `"floor_applied"` is written only when the floor actually lifted something; otherwise `"ml_predicted"`.

### The abstain threshold is derived at training time

`JOB_FIELD_CONFIDENCE_THRESHOLD` was a hardcoded 0.30, chosen when there were 8 classes. At 11 classes it was silently wrong: it discarded 139 of 368 *correct* held-out predictions and prevented zero errors, and it suppressed the correct field on half the real test emails. The threshold was discarding correct predictions.

The quantity being thresholded is a softmax over raw `LinearSVC` margins. It is not a probability and its scale depends on the margins a particular model happened to learn, so any fixed constant can become stale when the data, class count or estimator changes.

`ml/train.py` now derives it. It builds a no-signal probe — the real template bodies with every field slot filled from words belonging to no field — measures the trained model's confidence on them, and takes the 95th percentile. This measures how confident the model is on inputs with no field signal. The value is written to `ml/models/job_field_threshold.json` beside the pickle and read by `ml_service` at import, with a constant fallback and a warning when the file is absent.

This cannot be tuned against held-out accuracy instead, because the synthetic `job_field` head scores near 1.0 — every threshold discards only correct answers and prevents no errors. The no-signal probe is the only measurable reference point available.

Measured on the shipped model (56 probe emails, chance = 1/11 = 0.091):

| distribution                   | min   | median | p95       | max   |
| ------------------------------ | ----- | ------ | --------- | ----- |
| no-signal probe                | 0.104 | 0.123  | **0.167** | 0.180 |
| real emails (all four correct) | 0.230 | 0.304  | —         | 0.463 |

The derived value is **0.167**, and abstentions on the real emails went from 2/4 to 0/4. It differs from the 0.18 measured by hand before the retrain because the retrain moved the margin scale, which demonstrates why the value is derived per model.

## Consequences

Held out by whole template across 154 groups (30 groups, 368 rows), `category` scores 0.923 macro F1, `job_field` 0.974 and `urgency` 1.000. The nearest-neighbour probe between test and train sits at 0.288 with nothing above 0.70, so the held-out set is genuinely unseen rather than paraphrase. On the four real emails: `job_field` 4/4 — previously all four collapsed to `general` — `category` 3/4 and `urgency` 3/4.

The leak check, which asserts that no token exclusive to the name and company pools appears in any class's top 15 coefficients, now passes for all three heads. `urgency` was the last to fail; its top features are now marker n-grams (`confirm by`, `tomorrow`, `earliest convenience`, `no deadline`, `pressure either`) where they were previously `harborstone`, `james`, `thanks marcus` and `regards john`.

Retraining changed which estimator backs which head, and that exposed a latent coupling: `ml_service.predict()` hardcoded `predict_proba` for category and urgency and `decision_function` for job_field. When selection chose `LinearSVC` for the category head, every prediction raised `AttributeError`, the broad `except` swallowed it, and the service returned an empty result for every message. `_get_confidence()` now dispatches on `hasattr(model, "predict_proba")`. Per-head estimator assumptions should not be hardcoded: the trainer's purpose is to pick the best model per target, so which estimator wins is not knowable in advance.

No data migration accompanies the `"general"` → `"unclassified"` sentinel change; the existing analysed messages were deleted rather than migrated.

### Limits of this guarantee

* **The synthetic scores are uninformative, and this is the central limitation.** `urgency` at 1.000 and `job_field` at 0.974 measure tasks that are separable by construction — the urgency markers are near-deterministic keywords, and each field's role titles and technologies are disjoint. This is the same shape as the old `category` head at 1.000 offline and 0.35 on real mail. Neither number predicts real-world accuracy, and treating them as evidence would recreate the same evaluation mistake.

* **Four hand-labelled emails is not an evaluation set.** `train.py` prints the real-email block with a warning to that effect on every run and will keep doing so below 25 rows. One of the four labels is still wrong in the model's favour: the Harborstone scheduling email is predicted `high` when no deadline is stated, so `medium` is correct.

* **The evaluation set is hand-labelled by one person, and one label was corrected during this work.** The Redwood invitation was labelled `high` under the old category-driven scheme; applying the new definition strictly it asks for action but states no reply deadline — the interview date is not a due date — so it is `medium`. The model was right and the label was wrong. A single labeller with no adjudication is a limitation of the only real evidence in the system.

* **`category` still flags memorisation.** The permuted-group control scores 1.000 against 0.877 grouped, a gap of +0.123, meaning the head generalises across templates less well than an ungrouped split would suggest. This is unchanged in character by this work and was not something it targeted.

* **Urgency remains correlated with category by design.** `low` is unreachable for invitations, scheduling and offers; `high` is unreachable for rejections and acknowledgements. A real email violating those assumptions — a rejection demanding an urgent response — is outside the training distribution entirely.

* **The derived threshold is only as good as the probe.** The no-signal emails are built from the same templates as the training data, so they measure confidence on *in-distribution text with the field signal removed*, not on genuinely foreign email. A real email that is out-of-distribution in structure as well as vocabulary may score differently from anything the probe covers.

* **The abstain threshold and the confidence displayed are still uncalibrated.** The Field badge deliberately shows no percentage for `job_field` because the underlying number is a softmax over raw margins. Deriving the threshold makes it track the scale; it does not make the quantity a probability. Calibration (`CalibratedClassifierCV`) remains a separate, unstarted piece of work.
